Expired credentials are a revenue problem

5 min readAug 2026

A certification lapse looks like an HR problem until the claim for a visit delivered during that lapse comes back denied. At that point it is a revenue problem, and the revenue is usually not recoverable.

75.2% typical license compliance rate, unmanaged

Why payers care about certification status

Medicaid Waiver and other regulated payers generally require caregivers to hold current, verified credentials to bill for the visits they deliver. A visit delivered by a caregiver whose background check or certification had lapsed, even briefly, is frequently ineligible for reimbursement regardless of how well the care itself was delivered.

The gap between when it lapses and when it is caught is where the cost lives

Without active tracking, a certification expiry is often discovered only when a claim comes back denied, weeks after the visits in question were delivered. By then, every visit that caregiver delivered during the lapse is exposed, not just the one that triggered the denial. Agencies without active tracking commonly find compliance rates well below what they assumed.

Tracking needs to be proactive, not reactive

The fix is a system that flags a certification approaching expiry with enough lead time to renew it before the deadline, not one that surfaces the problem only after a claim is denied. A rolling report of every caregiver's certification status, checked weekly against expiry dates, catches lapses while they are still preventable.

This is a finance review item, not just an HR one

Certification tracking is usually owned by HR, and revenue-at-risk from lapsed credentials is a financial figure. Bringing the two together, so certification status is reviewed as part of the monthly finance close, alongside AR aging and payer margin, treats it as the revenue exposure it actually is, not just a compliance checklist.
